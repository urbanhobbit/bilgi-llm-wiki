## Overview

The 19 November 2026 edition of the EDMO Weekly Pulse dissects the information chaos that followed the 7 November mass shooting in Kraków, Poland, in which a 24-year-old killed six people and injured 15 before dying of his wounds, after apparently killing his family. The editorial shows how a familiar playbook was applied: the perpetrator was alternately claimed to be Ukrainian (serving Russian and domestic anti-refugee narratives) and a left-wing/LGBTQ+ activist (serving far-right actors on both sides of the Atlantic), while Elon Musk personally amplified debunked claims to his 240 million followers and X — unlike Meta and TikTok — reportedly did not respond to a single Polish report about the content. ZOOM-IN gathers three hub investigations into fabricated "evidence" (NORDIS, EDMO Ireland, HDMO), including AI-modified images with SynthID watermarks. ELECTION BEAT follows Russian operation "Matryoshka" targeting Spain's election and IBERIFIER's first two weekly monitoring reports. GLOBAL PULSE covers the "CO₂ saturation" myth, Black Sea drone-attack narratives in Romania and Bulgaria, and leaked documents on pro-Kremlin propagandist J.M. Dougan; ON A DIFFERENT NOTE features CEDMO's special brief on the risks of a digital society.

## ON THE RISE — How disinformation exploited the Kraków tragedy to attack the usual targets, and how Elon Musk made things worse

On 7 November 2026 a 24-year-old Polish man, Michał Oksiński, drove into Kraków's historic Main Market Square and opened fire on the crowd. Six people were killed and 15 injured, including two police officers. The attacker was wounded in an exchange of gunfire with police and died in hospital on 9 November. The same day, the bodies of his parents and 21-year-old sister were found at the family home in Ostrowiec Świętokrzyski; investigators believe he was responsible for their deaths.

These are the facts. Who Oksiński was and why he acted quickly became subjects of speculation and disinformation. Demagog.pl debunked numerous demonstrably false claims about his alleged identity, ideology, political views and affiliations: that he was not Polish but Ukrainian, that he had at least fought in Ukraine, that he wore Ukrainian patches during the attack; that he was an LGBTQ+ activist who had joined Pride demonstrations, or a leftist who had taken part in anti-racist marches. Shortly after the attack Polish Interior Minister Marcin Kierwiński suggested Oksiński might have been fascinated by far-right ideologies; later evidence pointed that way — online searches relating to Anders Behring Breivik and far-right symbols — but the investigation is ongoing, and statements about ideology without solid evidence, as the minister made immediately after the attack, only added noise to an already chaotic information environment.

The dynamics observed in Poland are a recurring phenomenon: whenever a tragic mass shooting occurs, online factions exploit it to attack their targets and political adversaries, rapidly circulating false content to support baseless theories. In Kraków, claims that the shooter was Ukrainian or linked to Ukraine served both domestic actors exploiting tensions between Poles and the large Ukrainian refugee community, and foreign actors — Russia in particular — which consistently spread disinformation targeting Ukraine to undermine European support for Kyiv. Portraying him as LGBTQ+ or left-wing served far-right forces on both sides of the Atlantic.

The role of X and Elon Musk was more than evident. Community Notes repeatedly fail to counter systemic circulation of false information, and the Kraków claims were no exception: the vast majority circulated widely and for a long time without notes. According to Polish authorities, X is the only major platform that — unlike Meta or TikTok — "has not responded to a single report regarding illegal and disinformation content" related to the shooting. Musk actively amplified demonstrably false content supporting the conspiracy that Oksiński was a leftist, including the claim about anti-racist marches; he amplified a post from a well-known conspiracy account claiming the BBC had described the incident as a "far-right shooting" despite comments allegedly identifying the attacker as a far-left radical and communist sympathiser, accusing the broadcaster of propaganda; and he shared another false claim portraying the attacker as a left-wing extremist who supported abortion rights and the LGBTQ+ community while hostile to Christianity.

## ZOOM-IN — A Closer Look at Cases Detected by the EDMO Network

In the Weekly Pulse of 30 April, EDMO examined how mass shootings can trigger harmful information trends — political manipulation, identity-based scapegoating, discrimination of vulnerable groups and the global spread of "crime" digital sub-cultures. The Kraków shooting of 7 November confirms these trends, with far-right actors, pro-Kremlin sources and social-media commentators exploiting the tragedy to spread competing narratives across borders about the perpetrator's alleged politics or Ukrainian connections.

### Unfounded rumours spread about the motive behind the mass murder in Poland (NORDIS)

Immediately after the shooting, Polish officials publicly commented on Oksiński's alleged links to right-wing extremism, referring to his interest in Anders Breivik. Nevertheless, claims of left-wing extremism spread widely on social media. According to Källkritikbyrån's analysis these rested in particular on a photo taken at an anti-racist demonstration in Łódź in March 2026 allegedly showing Oksiński. Independent fact-checks established that the person in the photograph was someone else and traced circulation of the claim and image to Herman Tertsch, a former Spanish journalist based in Warsaw and now an MEP for Spain's far-right Vox. In a parallel GADMO investigation, AFP confirmed that the young man holding a "Refugees Welcome" banner at the March 2026 Łódź demonstration is not Oksiński but another person, identified as Maksymilian, who told AFP he intends to take legal action over the disinformation spread about him. The investigation is ongoing and it is too early to draw conclusions.

### AI-modified images spread as "evidence" of the Polish shooter's political beliefs (EDMO Ireland)

The Journal's investigation examines how Irish far-right actors spread unfounded claims about Oksiński's political affiliations, focusing on Derek Blighe, founder of the far-right party Ireland First and a former election candidate. On 8 November Blighe published a Facebook post claiming Oksiński was a radical left-wing, antifascist and anarcho-communist activist; a collage of four images suggested the attack had been wrongly attributed to the far right. The post was shared thousands of times. The investigation found some images were over five years old and others manipulated with artificial intelligence, including images containing SynthID watermarks; the claims originated from an X account posting similar allegations in Polish. The images provide no reliable evidence of left-wing sympathies or political motives while investigations continue.

### Georg Spöttle also found the Ukrainian thread, sharing a suspicious AI image from a strange Telegram channel (HDMO)

Lakmusz's article analyses fabricated evidence supporting claims that Oksiński had links to Ukraine. Hungarian commentator Georg Spöttle shared a Facebook image of an armed, masked man in a vest bearing the Ukrainian flag, implying a connection with the perpetrator. Fact-checks showed the image was AI-generated or AI-manipulated. Moreover, the Telegram channel where it first appeared could not be reliably linked to Oksiński; on the contrary, the account's content appears to have been repeatedly modified, including after Oksiński's arrest and death. The article reconstructs how earlier insinuations about Ukrainian connections spread online, fuelled by sources close to Russian propaganda; according to a Polish investigation these narratives trace back to a pro-Russian disinformation operation aimed at implicating Ukraine in the tragedy.

## ELECTION BEAT — Tracking electoral disinformation through EDMO Hubs

### The Russian operation "Matryoshka" launches a campaign to interfere in Spain's upcoming election (IBERIFIER)

Maldita.es documented a new campaign targeting Spain's elections by the Russian disinformation operation Matryoshka, which seeks to influence public debate through coordinated false content and media impersonation. The campaign uses fabricated articles and videos resembling established Spanish outlets such as El País and El Mundo, and international ones such as AFP and The Economist, amplified through bot accounts on X and Bluesky. According to Antibot4Navalny, the goal is to present Spain's elections as ineffective, linking Europe's economic problems to support for Ukraine and sanctions on Russia, and advocating fragmentation including calls for Catalan independence. The content focuses mainly on Spanish domestic concerns, presenting closer ties to Russia and disengagement from the EU as solutions.

### Spain general election 2026: IBERIFIER's weekly report 1 (IBERIFIER)

IBERIFIER's first election-monitoring report, covering 2–6 November, identified misleading claims about Spain's upcoming elections. Several emerged after Transport Minister Óscar Puente resigned following a fatal train crash in Urda, causing confusion about candidate-replacement rules: false messages on X said electoral lists could not be modified after registration, or that voters had to cross out Puente's name for their vote to count — misleading interpretations of electoral rules. The report also notes AI-generated interference: a widely shared image mimicking an El País front page with polling showing a huge PSOE rise, and another fabricated image of an allegedly empty campaign rally.

### Spain general election 2026: IBERIFIER's weekly report 2 (IBERIFIER)

In its second report, IBERIFIER identified three possible signals to the integrity of Spain's electoral process in the week of 7–13 November, all still under review. That week also saw the launch of Comprobado 29N, an alliance of Spanish fact-checking organisations coordinating verification until 6 December. The report focuses mainly on voting procedures — misleading claims about ballot validity and messages impersonating official voting notifications — plus decontextualised or recycled content (outdated information on public funding and political affiliations) and misleading figures on immigration, gender equality, LGBTI rights and alleged foreign links of parties.

## GLOBAL PULSE — Disinformation narratives shaping the world's conversations

### The atmosphere is not "saturated"; excess CO₂ continues to cause warming (BENEDMO)

Can the atmosphere become "saturated" with CO₂, so that additional emissions no longer cause warming? A Factcheck.Vlaanderen fact-check says no, debunking claims promoted by the US-based Heartland Institute, which has a history of challenging the scientific consensus on climate change, opposing environmental policies and being financially linked to donors with fossil-fuel interests including ExxonMobil. Climate scientists cited in the fact-check explain that rising CO₂ concentrations continue to strengthen the greenhouse effect: while CO₂ already absorbs much infrared radiation at certain wavelengths, additional CO₂ still makes it harder for heat to escape into space. The "CO₂ saturation" theory is not supported by current climate science.

### Narratives from the Black Sea: drone attacks trigger battle over narratives in Romania and Bulgaria (BROD)

Between 5 and 23 October 2026 drone attacks on commercial vessels in the Black Sea triggered competing narratives across Romania and Bulgaria. A report by BROD partner Funky Citizens examines how the information environment evolved, analysing claims about who was to blame, whether the ships were legitimate targets, NATO's ability to provide security and the risk of further escalation. Pro-Kremlin narratives rapidly adapted to the attacks, promoting unsupported claims before official information was available; delays in coordinated institutional responses created opportunities for those narratives to gain traction and shape public perceptions of the unfolding security crisis.

### Leaked documents reveal how pro-Kremlin propagandist J.M. Dougan was used and discarded by Russian intelligence (IDMO)

Leaked documents published by The Insider offer new insight into John Mark Dougan, a former Florida deputy sheriff who fled to Moscow in 2016. According to the investigation, Dougan had grown increasingly frustrated with his role in Russian influence operations, complaining about workload, low pay and the constraints of working in Russia. The files also shed light on the mechanics of his disinformation activity, linking him to false allegations against former US vice-presidential candidate Tim Walz, a network of 167 fake local news websites and efforts to spread false narratives about Ukraine — largely reinforcing earlier reporting on his role in pro-Kremlin campaigns.
