# Mike Males Substack: Secondary Analysis of US Health Agency Surveys on Adolescent Well-being

**Source:** [https://mikemales.substack.com/p/our-top-health-agencys-surveys-found](https://mikemales.substack.com/p/our-top-health-agencys-surveys-found)

## Summary

Mike Males' Substack analytical piece examines data from official US health agency surveys concerning adolescent well-being trends, applying critical epidemiological and secondary data analysis methodology to publicly available government statistics to generate alternative interpretations of established narratives. The analysis employs rigorous secondary data analysis methodology, systematically re-examining publicly available survey data from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention's Youth Risk Behavior Surveillance System (YRBSS), the National Survey on Drug Use and Health, and other federal health surveillance systems. The methodological approach involves comprehensive trend analysis of multiple adolescent health indicators over extended time periods, systematically comparing patterns across demographic subgroups including age, gender, race and ethnicity, and socioeconomic indicators, and critically examining correlations between adolescent well-being measures and social, economic, and technological changes including the rise of social media and smartphones. The analysis critically evaluates important methodological issues in health surveillance data systems including the significant effects of question wording changes across survey waves that can create artificial trends, sampling frame coverage limitations that may systematically exclude certain populations, and the potential for ecological fallacy in interpreting aggregate population-level trends as reflecting individual-level experiences and causal processes. The piece effectively demonstrates how rigorous secondary analysis of well-established government survey data can generate alternative and sometimes contradictory interpretations to official narratives and widely publicized claims, highlighting the crucial importance of methodological transparency, analytical rigor, and critical thinking in health research and its public communication. While the piece is published on a Substack platform rather than a traditional peer-reviewed academic venue, the analysis legitimately applies sound epidemiological and statistical methods to publicly accessible data sources, effectively demonstrating how sophisticated academic research skills can be productively applied to public scholarship and informed policy debate beyond the boundaries of traditional academic publishing. The methodological contribution lies primarily in its critical and independent re-engagement with existing survey data and its systematic identification of analytical blind spots, alternative interpretations, and methodological limitations in official health reporting that may not receive adequate attention in primary research publications or media coverage.

## Key Points

- Secondary analysis of CDC Youth Risk Behavior Surveillance System and other federal health survey data demonstrates critical re-engagement with government statistics
- Comprehensive trend analysis of adolescent health indicators across demographic subgroups reveals important patterns potentially masked by aggregate-level reporting
- Methodological critique of survey question wording changes across waves highlights the critical importance of measurement consistency in longitudinal surveillance research
- Ecological fallacy awareness reminds researchers that aggregate population trends may not accurately reflect individual-level experiences or causal processes
- Public scholarship using legitimate epidemiological and statistical methods demonstrates how academic analytical skills can productively inform public debate beyond traditional publishing
