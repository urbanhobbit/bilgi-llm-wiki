## Overview

The 16 July 2026 edition of the EDMO Weekly Pulse examines how disinformation creates "alternative facts" when reality contradicts preferred narratives, with a focus on the 2026 FIFA World Cup as a vector for racist and Islamophobic disinformation. The ZOOM-IN section features three fact-checks: FIFA's monitoring of racist abuse during the World Cup, unfounded match-fixing allegations against the Algeria–Austria match, and an AI-generated deepfake video of FIFA president Infantino. The GLOBAL PULSE covers AI-generated funeral imagery inflating support for Iran's former Supreme Leader, an old AI-manipulated photo shared by the US President as evidence of a strike on Iran, anti-NATO disinformation around the Ankara summit, and wildfire conspiracy theories targeting renewable energy projects in Spain. ON A DIFFERENT NOTE highlights the new BENEDMO white paper on AI's role in the disinformation landscape.

## ON THE RISE — So much the worse for the facts?

One of the most remarkable features of disinformation is its ability to maintain the appearance of being right even when reality proves otherwise. Russian disinformation routinely fabricates false evidence of NATO troops being present in Ukraine because the facts did not fit the narrative it wanted to advance ("Moscow is fighting against the whole Alliance"). US disinformation continues to repeat the claim that the European Union censors free speech, despite the State Department finding no evidence to support that claim after weeks of research. The pattern is always the same: "If the facts do not conform to the theory, so much the worse for the facts."

Domestic actors spreading disinformation use the same technique. Whether the topic is vaccines, climate change or racism, when reality disproves conspiracy theories, the internet and social media provide the perfect habitat for a parallel reality with "evidence" to prove it. The ongoing FIFA World Cup in the United States, particularly the match between France and Morocco — considered a "high risk" event — was too tempting an opportunity for those who spread racist and Islamophobic disinformation. Videos showing riots and violence in Paris circulated with misleading captions falsely linking them to France's victory, but no such unrest had taken place; the videos were old footage from unrelated events.

An unregulated information environment like social media — which, according to the Reuters Institute Digital News Report, has become the main source of news for many — is the perfect place to distort reality, especially with AI capable of generating increasingly realistic images and videos. Confirmation bias and cognitive laziness are evolutionary features that make us particularly vulnerable to disinformation and can even turn us into its unconscious amplifiers.

## ZOOM-IN

### Racism is the main form of abuse on social media during the World Cup according to FIFA

*Source: IBERIFIER*

Chequeado examines the rise of racist abuse during the 2026 FIFA World Cup. Racism was the most common form of discrimination detected by FIFA's Social Media Protection Service (SMPS). During the group stage, more than six million social media posts and comments were monitored, with 89,000 identified as abusive. Of these, 11% contained racist content, an increase from 8% during the equivalent stage of the 2022 World Cup. FIFA's moderation system, combining AI with human review, automatically hid more than 181,000 offensive comments and referred around 1,000 accounts for investigation. A novelty in this edition is data collection from security forces, identifying 100 legally attributable cases throughout the group stage. The article also discusses racist remarks by public officials including Argentine presidential adviser Santiago Caputo, Mendoza vice-governor Hebe Casado, and Paraguayan senator Celeste Amarilla targeting players such as Kylian Mbappé.

### Algeria-Austria match supposedly "fixed": why these unfounded accusations?

*Source: DE FACTO*

Unfounded allegations that the 3–3 draw between Algeria and Austria in the final round of the 2026 FIFA World Cup group stage was "fixed" circulated widely. Because the result allowed both teams to qualify, numerous social media users claimed without evidence that the two federations had colluded. Posts on X linked a video of an on-field confrontation presented misleadingly as proof. Other posts on Facebook and Instagram presented a fabricated FIFA press release claiming an investigation had been opened. Many posts originated from Moroccan supporter accounts, suggesting a coordinated disinformation campaign. Investigative journalist Romain Molina explains that although match-fixing still exists, it is highly unlikely at the World Cup due to intense scrutiny and high stakes.

### Infantino did not say that the president of Mexico offered him money to win the World Cup: the video was created with AI

*Source: IBERIFIER*

A viral video claiming FIFA president Gianni Infantino said Mexico's president Claudia Sheinbaum offered him money to ensure Mexico won the 2026 FIFA World Cup is an AI-generated deepfake, according to Newtral's fact-check. The video fabricates both audio and apparent lip movements. In the clip, Infantino supposedly says he rejected the alleged bribe and that "the only World Cup winner is Messi." Forensic analysis using AI-detection tools (Hiya via InVid, Hive AI) indicated a high probability of artificially generated audio, with visuals digitally altered to match the fake speech. Reverse image searches traced the original footage to a genuine 2022 video in which Infantino discussed violence at an Argentine football match — not Mexico, Sheinbaum, or the World Cup.

## GLOBAL PULSE

### AI funeral images inflate support for Iran's former Supreme Leader

*Source: DE FACTO*

As Iran commemorated former Supreme Leader Ali Khamenei, widely shared images and videos purported to show enormous crowds gathering for funeral ceremonies. Fact-checkers found that many viral visuals had been generated or manipulated with AI to exaggerate attendance. Investigators at 20 minutes identified warning signs including unrealistic crowd patterns, perfectly aligned banners, altered landmarks, and inconsistencies in building layouts. Some videos showed impossible proportions, static crowds, or visual anomalies typical of AI-generated content.

### US President shares old, AI-manipulated photo as evidence of new US strike on Iran

*Source: MEDDMO*

A dramatic image shared online was neither recent nor authentic. Fact-checkers traced it to a 2025 photograph of a fire at an oil depot in Tehran, unrelated to the events it was used to depict. The photo was circulated by prominent accounts and reposted by US President Donald Trump. The image had been digitally altered with visible modifications compared to the original. Although the account that first shared it later acknowledged the mistake, the image had already spread widely and was used to support claims about ongoing military operations.

### Anti NATO disinformation surged around Ankara summit

*Source: BROD*

As NATO leaders gathered in Ankara in July 2026, researchers observed a sharp increase in anti-NATO narratives targeting Romanian audiences. Analysis of more than 1,000 articles and thousands of Facebook posts found content linked to the pro-Kremlin Pravda Romania network intensified significantly during the summit period. These outlets promoted consistent narratives: portraying NATO as responsible for escalating tensions, depicting the Alliance as internally divided, framing defence measures as provocations, and suggesting recent drone incidents in Romania were deliberate Ukrainian attacks. Similar claims appeared on mainstream Romanian media websites, giving disinformation narratives broader visibility during a key moment in European security debates.

### Wildfire conspiracy theories target renewable energy projects

*Source: IBERIFIER*

Following the devastating wildfire in Los Gallardos, Spain, social media posts claimed the blaze was deliberately set to clear the way for a large solar energy project. Fact-checkers found no evidence: the projects cited were either outside the affected area or no longer viable. The claims mirror a recurring narrative seen elsewhere in Europe. A recent Greek fact-check similarly debunked allegations that wind farms were being built on land destroyed by wildfires. In both cases, genuine renewable energy projects were falsely linked to major fires, turning public concerns about energy infrastructure into unsupported conspiracy theories.

## ON A DIFFERENT NOTE

> AI has not completely altered the fundamental logic of disinformation, but has significantly increased its scale, speed and accessibility. Deepfakes and AI-generated content are not consistently more convincing than traditional forms of disinformation, but generative tools make it easier and cheaper for a wide range of actors to produce and disseminate misleading content. (...) Only a long term, collective effort by platforms, media professionals, fact-checkers, researchers and policymakers — with continuous monitoring, systematic experimentation and adaptation of practices — can keep democratic information provision sufficiently resilient.
>
> — New BENEDMO white paper on the role of AI in the disinformation landscape
