# LSE Digital Futures for Children: Child Rights and Digital Regulation — Interdisciplinary Research Programme

**Source:** [https://www.lse.ac.uk/digital-futures-for-children/events/child-rights-regulation/home.aspx](https://www.lse.ac.uk/digital-futures-for-children/events/child-rights-regulation/home.aspx)

## Summary

The LSE Digital Futures for Children research programme's events and publications on child rights and digital regulation represent a significant and innovative methodological contribution to interdisciplinary childhood studies in digital contexts. The research programme employs a sophisticated multi-method framework combining several complementary methodological approaches: legal-doctrinal analysis systematically examining children's rights instruments including the UN Convention on the Rights of the Child and its General Comments, empirical qualitative research conducted directly with children and families using child-appropriate methods, comprehensive policy analysis of digital regulation frameworks across multiple jurisdictions, and comparative international legal methodology examining how different legal systems implement children's digital rights protections. The methodological approach centrally features child participatory research methods that fundamentally recognize children as active social agents with valuable perspectives rather than passive research subjects to be observed, employing creative and age-appropriate methods including drawing, photography, storytelling, and digital diaries alongside traditional interviews and focus groups. The framework rigorously operationalizes the UN Convention on the Rights of the Child as an analytical lens, translating its general principles including provision, protection, and participation rights into specific, measurable indicators through structured rights-based impact assessment methodology that can be applied to evaluate digital platforms, services, and regulatory frameworks. The research methodology incorporates genuinely innovative approaches to studying children's digital environments including participatory co-design workshops where children actively collaborate in developing research instruments and materials ensuring age-appropriateness and relevance, digital ethnography examining children's online spaces and practices in naturalistic contexts with appropriate ethical safeguards, and longitudinal tracking of regulatory impacts on children's digital experiences over time capturing dynamic effects as regulations evolve. The genuinely interdisciplinary methodology effectively bridges multiple academic disciplines including law, sociology, media and communication studies, developmental psychology, and computer science, demonstrating how the complex and multifaceted questions surrounding digital childhood inherently require methodological pluralism and cross-disciplinary collaboration rather than narrow disciplinary approaches. For researchers studying children's digital lives, this framework provides a comprehensive model for conducting rigorous, ethically sound, and child-centered research on children's digital rights that effectively centers children's own perspectives and experiences while maintaining appropriate academic analytical rigor and theoretical sophistication.

## Key Points

- Child participatory research methods fundamentally recognize children as active social agents with valuable perspectives in research about their digital lives
- UN Convention on the Rights of the Child provides a systematic analytical framework for structured rights-based impact assessment of digital regulation affecting children
- Genuinely interdisciplinary methodology bridges law, sociology, media studies, developmental psychology, and computer science in studying digital childhood
- Participatory co-design workshops with children for developing research instruments ensure age-appropriateness, relevance, and ethical data collection
- Digital ethnography and longitudinal regulatory impact tracking provide comprehensive understanding of how digital environments shape children's experiences over time
