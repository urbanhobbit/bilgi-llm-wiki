## Overview

The 24 July 2026 edition of the EDMO Weekly Pulse focuses on the EU's Rapid Response System (RRS) for election integrity, addressing misinformation claiming it is a censorship tool. The issue also examines electoral disinformation patterns across Hungary, Bulgaria, Cyprus, and Malta, a Russian campaign targeting German elections, and global narratives around Iran, Ukraine, climate change, and the Falklands. This is the final issue before the summer break; the Weekly Pulse will return on 27 August 2026.

## ON THE RISE — Weekly Watch of Emerging Disinformation Risks

### EDMO defending election integrity via the RRS: True transparency and censorship nonsense

The Rapid Response System (RRS) of the EU's Code of Practice on Disinformation (now the Code of Conduct on Disinformation) is a cooperation and communication mechanism that enables the rapid sharing of information between online platforms and non-platform stakeholders during elections. Its purpose is to identify and discuss potentially harmful disinformation trends that could threaten the integrity of electoral processes in the EU.

Clearly, it is not an instrument of censorship. Any decision about removing, labeling, or downgrading content is entirely up to the platforms. It is their responsibility, and no one else's.

Until recently, however, RRS's nature and functioning were not entirely clear to the public, because social media platforms insisted on sharing as little information as possible outside the Taskforce of the Code of Practice, whose activities and discussions are covered by confidentiality. This uncertainty created a grey area where legitimate concerns, unfounded speculation, and outright lies thrived.

In particular, the ongoing disinformation campaign by the U.S. administration, and specifically by the House Judiciary Committee chaired by Jim Jordan, about the EU's regulation of the information space (the Code of Practice but also the Digital Services Act, or DSA) benefited from the lack of sufficient, clear, and publicly available information. The attacks from across the Atlantic prompted civil society organisations that are members of the Code of Practice Taskforce to take a firmer stance and call for greater transparency and accountability.

This push has led to more information about the RRS becoming publicly available and, for EDMO, to the drafting of strict guidelines that ensure, among other things, a better use of the RRS, more closely aligned with journalistic standards.

The EDMO guidelines provide, regarding the transparent use of the RRS, that the EDMO Hubs involved should publicly announce their participation in the system and explain what it is and what it does; that the notifications sent to platforms should also be published on EDMO websites; and that, after the vote, an ex post analysis of the RRS's performance should be published on EDMO websites. These recommendations were followed, for example, during the Hungarian, Maltese, and Cypriot elections and facilitated a more transparent use of the RRS, as well as more consistent monitoring of the electoral cycle.

The nature and scope of the RRS should not be misinterpreted, even now that more information is publicly available. The work of civil society organisations within the RRS cannot compensate for the deficiencies of platforms in ensuring compliance with EU and national laws and regulations. The RRS is primarily useful for testing the performance of the platforms, highlighting shortcomings, and providing useful information to address these issues. This objective should be pursued with particular determination ahead of the busy electoral year of 2027, when France, Italy, Spain, Poland, and many other EU countries will hold major elections.

## ZOOM-IN — A Closer Look at Cases Detected by the EDMO Network

The following analysis and fact-checking articles offer a closer look at how electoral disinformation unfolds in practice. The most recent investigations from MedDMO and NORDIS illustrate some of the forms electoral information manipulation can take — coordinated domestic influence operations in Malta and Russian disinformation targeting upcoming länder elections in Germany — confirming the broader cross-country patterns identified by EDMO in its analysis of the past elections in Hungary, Bulgaria, Malta and Cyprus.

### A comparative analysis of electoral disinformation in Hungary, Bulgaria, Cyprus and Malta

Four general elections across Hungary, Bulgaria, Cyprus and Malta revealed distinct but increasingly sophisticated information battlegrounds, according to EDMO's analysis of the Spring 2026 electoral cycle. While each country faced unique vulnerabilities, a common pattern emerged: electoral manipulation has shifted away from isolated falsehoods towards coordinated campaigns combining artificial intelligence, opaque political advertising, algorithmic amplification and operational capabilities of foreign actors.

Hungary experienced the most extensive manipulation, with AI-generated propaganda, coordinated fake social media networks and Russian-linked influence operations supporting pro-government narratives. Bulgaria, by contrast, saw limited AI use but extensive coordinated activity on Telegram and pro-Kremlin campaigns designed to undermine trust in democratic institutions rather than promote specific parties. Cyprus faced a mixture of domestic political disinformation, anonymous advertising networks and Chinese state-backed influence, while Malta's largely domestic campaigns centred on AI-generated content, misleading political advertising and polarising rhetoric.

The analysis also found that internationally recognisable narratives spread easily across borders, whereas locally focused controversies remained confined to national audiences. Across all four elections, social media platforms emerged as critical vulnerabilities, with inadequate advertising transparency, limited researcher access to platform data and largely reactive content moderation hampering oversight.

### Russian campaign spreads false claims in videos impersonating well-known media ahead of German elections

Nordis reports that German authorities have identified a Russian-linked disinformation campaign aimed at influencing the upcoming state election in Saxony-Anhalt. Since early July, the campaign has circulated fabricated claims about candidates from the Greens, CDU, SPD and Die Linke through short videos distributed by a coordinated bot network on X. To make the content appear credible, the videos misuse the logos and branding of well-known German media outlets, including Süddeutsche Zeitung, FAZ, Spiegel TV and Die Zeit. Germany's domestic intelligence agency (BfV) says the operation represents unlawful foreign information manipulation intended to affect the election outcome.

Notably, the far-right AfD has reportedly not been targeted by false stories, potentially allowing it to benefit indirectly. Authorities believe the activity forms part of the long-running Russian influence campaign known as "Operation Matryoshka", which seeks to amplify fabricated content by prompting journalists and media organisations to investigate or inadvertently spread it.

### Operation Apostle: An Influence Operation Targeting Malta's 2026 General Election

MedDMO has carried out a deeper analysis of "Operation Apostle", a coordinated online influence campaign that allegedly sought to shape public opinion ahead of Malta's general election in May 2026. Researchers identified a network of interconnected websites, Facebook pages and online domains that appeared shortly before the election and consistently promoted narratives favourable to the governing Labour Party while attacking the opposition Nationalist Party. The operation combined AI-assisted content with locally informed messaging, suggesting human oversight rather than fully automated production.

Investigators found links between sites such as Appostli, Good Morning Malta and other recently registered domains, which recycled news content, amplified partisan themes and purchased social media advertisements using obscure payment identities. The report highlights evidence of coordinated behaviour, including suspicious engagement patterns and troll-like activity, while noting that definitive attribution remains unproven.

## GLOBAL PULSE — Disinformation narratives shaping the world's conversations

### Iran Damaged US Bases in the Middle East, but Claims of Total Destruction Are Overstated

Posts circulating online claimed that 27 US military bases in the Middle East had been reduced to ashes by Iranian attacks. The Romanian fact-check (BROD) found this to be partly false: Iranian strikes did cause significant damage to US military facilities, but not on the scale suggested by viral claims. The misleading posts combined real military setbacks with exaggerated figures and unsupported claims.

### Misleading Maps Exaggerate Russian Gains in Ukraine

Social media posts claimed that Russia had made its largest territorial advances in months, citing analysis by the battlefield mapping project DeepState. However, the posts selectively reported only part of the findings. DeepState's assessment noted Russian gains in June but also found that, once Ukrainian advances were taken into account, Russia had lost more ground than it gained overall.

### Heatwave Conspiracies Blame Solar Flares Instead of Climate Change

As record temperatures swept across Europe and North America, viral social media posts claimed that the heat was caused by solar flares, solar storms, or even directed-energy technologies, rather than climate change. Scientists reject these claims: solar flares do not affect temperatures at Earth's surface and cannot produce the kind of heatwaves seen in 2026.

### World Cup Match Revives Falklands Sovereignty Debate

Ahead of the 2026 World Cup semi-final between Argentina and England, an exchange on social media between Argentine Foreign Minister Pablo Quirno and former Thatcher adviser Nile Gardiner reignited debate over the Falkland Islands/Malvinas. After Argentina defeated England, players celebrated with a banner reading "Las Malvinas son Argentinas", prompting calls in the UK for a FIFA investigation.
