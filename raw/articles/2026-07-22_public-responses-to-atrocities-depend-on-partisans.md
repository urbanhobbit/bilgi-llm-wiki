# Public responses to atrocities depend on partisanship and context, not legal labels

**Author(s):** Y. Lelkes, E. M. Mitchell, E. Walk, S. J. Westwood

**Published:** 2026

**Source:** [https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2608203123](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2608203123)

## Summary

Legal labels such as genocide and crimes against humanity carry substantial legal and diplomatic weight in elite and institutional arenas. This study investigates whether these labels shape public opinion about atrocities, measuring support for punitive policies (sanctions, boycotts), humanitarian responses (refugee admissions), and moral judgments (sympathy, collective responsibility). Using survey experiments, the authors isolate label effects from country context and partisanship. Public-opinion evidence on atrocity labels is limited; one study had found that calling a conflict genocide can decrease perceived collective responsibility, but used a single hypothetical label. The current study tests whether effects generalize to other legal terms, outcomes, and real-world contexts, examining whether sensitivity to labels concentrates where priors are weaker or instead produces backlash.

## Key Points

- Partisanship and context drive public responses to atrocities more than legal labels (genocide, crimes against humanity)
- Survey experiments measure punitive, humanitarian, and moral responses separately
- Label effects isolated from country context and partisanship in experimental design
- Tests whether label sensitivity concentrates in low-priority domains or produces backlash
- Relevant to disinformation around atrocity classification and polarized public discourse
