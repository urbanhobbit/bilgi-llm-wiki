# Further thoughts on partisanship and polarization after the midterm elections

**Andrew Gelman** — *Statistical Modeling, Causal Inference, and Social Science*, 26 Kasım 2026.

Orijinal metin (blog RSS akışından):

I wanted to revisit some things that came up after the 2026 elections:

1. The migration of tech investors from center-left to far right

After seeing the movie You Can See Everything, I noted that the disgraced medtech fraudster Elizabeth Holmes and her husband had positioned themselves firmly on the right side of the political spectrum, expressing support for Donald Trump and Charlie Kirk.  I wrote:

If all this had happened fifteen to thirty years ago, I’m guessing that all these people would’ve been Democrats. Big tech money is right-coded today but was left-coded in the (Bill) Clinton and Obama eras.

The question is, why has this happened? The trend is particularly notable when juxtaposed with the general shift of college-educated voters away from the Republican party during the past two decades.

Various answers came up, including the very obvious one that Holmes is in federal prison, so she’s naturally following the get-a-pardon-from-Trump playbook.  But I think it’s more than that, given that various leading tech investors who aren’t in federal prison are also supporting the far right.

Tom Ferguson wrote:

Tech industries are now much more intertwined with the military. But there was a very significant segment of it that was always so: Peter Thiel is the most famous and very important, but he wasn’t alone. I’d accept the standard narrative that he profited a lot from support from CIA venture capital in Silicon Valley; the agency and perhaps other interested parties had set up special funds there. So did some other now in the news.  After c. 2017 the connections got much tighter and broader, as the US began to break openly with China and vice versa. I actually told this story compactly in an interview very soon after the 2024 election.

That all makes sense.  But I also think there was something else going on.  In G W Bush’s second term, there was a sense that the Republican party was ideological and incompetent in both foreign and economic policy, while Obama and the Democrats were competent and largely non-ideological:  kind of like Bill Clinton without the social-issues baggage, the party of the future, both in terms of economic policy and the demographics of the voters.  From that perspective, allying with the Democrats was like jumping on a moving train, getting in on the ground floor with the future party of government.  Also, though, I think that Obama and the Democrats represented economic, political, and social stability.

But after 2010 and then 2016, two things happened.  First, electoral reversals gave a sense that the two parties were equally balanced, which suggested that at the very least it was a good idea for tech leaders to spread their bets and not tie themselves to the Democrats.  Hence it didn’t seem so bad for some prominent business leaders to support Trump, as a kind of industry-wide hedge.  Second, Democratic rule did not seem to be leading to the anticipated political stability.  Obama (like Clinton) promised to rise over party, and he didn’t.  Even if this was the fault of Republicans and their allies for offering such a fierce opposition, the result was that the continuation of the “long 1990s” didn’t happen, which led some business leaders to seek alternatives.  Then after 2020 and 2024 there was a sense that the Republicans were playing hardball and the Democrats weren’t:  if you don’t loudly support Trump, he will punish you, but the Democrats wouldn’t do that.

2.  A potential for partisan depolarization

The other day I conjectured that the 2026 midterm election results could lead to a welcome decline in partisan polarization in this country.

In the week since then, we’ve seen a near-abdication of initiative from the formerly-dominant Republican party (an example being their recent attempt to blame the FAA flight stoppage on . . . transgender people in sports), and this got me thinking about how this might relate to a hoped-for depolarization.

My thought is that the timing could be right for depolarization, in a way that wasn’t possible in the other recent incumbent-repudiation midterms of 1994, 2006, 2010, and 2018.

Here’s my argument.  In 1994, the Republicans were still pulling themselves off the mat after their shellacking by Bill Clinton two years earlier.  Their main political project in Congress was to shut Clinton down—literally shutting down the government (a then-new political tactic) and also denying him any legislative victories.  Congressional Republicans didn’t succeed at stopping the president, who easily won reelection and left office at a high level of popularity—but they did succeed in setting a new standard in party unity (which is related to partisan polarization but sounds much more positive), and that served them well during the next two decades.

In 2006, my take on the Democrats is that they were in the ascendant and that George W. Bush was on the ropes, so they felt no need to engage in cross-party compromise.  As the party of the future, they could wait for their rewards to come.

At this point, I recognize that my argument has a certain heads-I-win, tails-you-lose unfalsifiability: I’m crediting the no-compromise position of Congressional Republicans after 1994 to their political insecurity, but then crediting the Democrats’ similar stance after 2006 to their overweening confidence.  And maybe my storytelling here does have a problem, I’m not sure.  But bear with me.

The next two incumbent repudiations were the Tea Party in 2010 and the anti-Trump resistance in 2018.  One reason these didn’t lead to depolarization was that presidents Obama and Trump were in no mood to compromise, with each being at the beginning of their first term.  It takes two to depolarize.

But now things are different.

The Trump administration has run out of energy as well as popular support and is just reacting to events, and lockstep part unity doesn’t seem like much of a solution for congressional Republicans, as they don’t have any obvious bounce-back to anticipate for the next elections.

On the other side of the aisle, the Democratic party in Congress is in an interesting position. Unlike the congressional Republicans in 1994 and 2010 or the Democrats in 2018, they don’t have an active president to push against, so they can’t just define themselves as oppositional.  For the Tea Party after 2010 or the resistance Dems after 2018, shutting down the administration was a goal in itself, and they could judge themselves successful to the extent that they stopped the government in its tracks.  That’s not enough for the Democrats today:  they’re expected not just to oppose Trump but to craft new policies.

The other part of the picture is potential depolarization within each party.  The newly-elected Democratic congressmembers are still mostly in the happy-talk phase, but we hear big debates in the pundit class between moderates and leftists.  Meanwhile, some congressional Republicans have been jumping off the Trump train, now that the president’s electoral power seems to have dissipated.

Put this together and I’m hoping we could see some splits within each party—with different splits happening on different issues (economics, foreign policy, health care, crypto/gambling/AI, etc.)—and a range of cross-party coalitions.  Ideally, legislators should be able to take independent positions without the fear that “wedge issues” will shatter their party.

A big factor I haven’t been considering is $.  The tech investors couldn’t buy the 2026 election but with their money and political influence they might be able to pay off enough Republicans and Democrats in Congress to get what they want.  Relevant here is this post by Tom Ferguson on the different patterns of campaign donations to the two contenders for Senate Majority Leader.

3.  Timing in politics

To continue with point 2 above (not the $, but the idea that the time could be ripe for a decrease in partisan polarization), I want to emphasize the importance of timing, in particular the phases of economic and business cycles.

Back in January, 2010, I wrote a post that I still stand by, called It’s not 1933, it’s 1930:

A major storyline of the 2008 election was that it was the Great Depression all over again: George W. Bush was the hapless Herbert Hoover and Barack Obama was the FDR figure, coming in on a wave of popular resentment to clean things up. The stock market crash made the parallels pretty direct. One could continue the analogy, with Bill Clinton playing the Calvin Coolidge role, mindlessly stoking the paper economy and complicit in the rise of the stock market as a national sport. Public fascination with various richies seemed very 1920s-ish, and we had lots of candidates for the “Andrew Mellon” of the 2000s. Obama’s decisive victory echoed Roosevelt’s in 1932.

But history doesn’t really repeat itself—or if it does, it’s not always quite the repetition that was expected. With his latest plan of a spending freeze (on the 17% of the federal budget that is not committed to the military, veterans, homeland security and international affairs, Social Security, or Medicare), Obama is being labeled by many liberals as the second coming of Herbert Hoover–another well-meaning technocrat who can’t put together a political coalition to do anything to stop the slide. Conservatives, too, may have switched from thinking of Obama as a scary realigning Roosevelt to viewing him as a Hoover from their own perspective–as a well-meaning fellow who took a stock market crash and made it worse through a series of ill-timed government interventions. . . .

In either storyline, it’s 1930, not 1932: rather than being three years into a depression, we’re still just getting started and we’re still in the Hoover-era position of seeing things fall apart but not quite being ready to take the next step.

Anyway, I’m not claiming to offer any serious political or economic analysis here, just pointing out that the 1932 election was a full three years after the 1929 stock market crash, so Obama’s stepping into the story at a different point than when Roosevelt stepped in to his.

Today is not a repeat of 1930 or 1933:  There are fears of an economic crash but it hasn’t quite happened yet.  In this sense, the auspicious timing I see is political:  the combination of an exhausted government with a large congressional majority among the opposition that could provide an opening for cross-party cooperation.  There’s one wild card in the deck, though, and that’s the very partisan Supreme Court.  We’ll see what happens.
